# Mente Design Review Ledger

The open problems in Mente's design, found in the review of 2026-11-04, and what is proposed for each. The design is corrected one entry at a time: an entry is decided, the governing `spec/*.md` and the examples are changed to match, and the entry's status moves to `done` with the commit that did it.

The first pass, on 2026-11-04, made the documents say what the compiler does: every `Implemented` label that described a plan was relabelled, names of functions and types that do not exist were removed, and the measured numbers replaced the claimed ones. That pass changed no design decision. The entries below are the design decisions it found; between 2026-11-04 and 2026-11-05 each was decided with Norman and applied in its own commit, except those still marked `proposed` or `open`.

A second review on 2026-11-06 read the design, the examples and `docs/design-brief.md` against the code at `bdffc1c`, and added the `open` and `proposed` entries of sections 1 to 9. The same day Norman set Mente's goal, a general-purpose language for AI agents (section 10), and the long-term order of the work: foundation, self-hosting, performance, new domains (`long-term-order` and the entries after it in section 8). Apart from `long-term-order`, which is `decided`, none of these entries was decided that day; each decision since is recorded in its entry's status, with the date.

Statuses: `open` (not decided), `proposed` (a resolution is written below and waits for a decision), `decided` (agreed, not yet applied), `done` (applied; the commit is named).

Rules for entries, decided by Norman on 2026-11-13:

- **A decision names what would reopen it** ([`reversal-conditions`](design-review.md#reversal-conditions)). A decision is recorded in its resolution as `**Decided (Norman, YYYY-MM-DD).**`. An entry decided on or after 2026-11-13 ends its resolution with `**Reopen if:**` and the evidence that would reopen it, one sentence that opens with a capital letter, with nothing after it in the cell: an observation the project's own instruments can make, with a threshold where one makes sense, such as "This rule is behind more than a tenth of the repairs in a milestone's benchmark.", or "None: it follows from an invariant." (`philosophy.md`, "What does not change"). A judgment, such as "if it proves awkward", is no condition, since it can never fire. Each milestone review checks the conditions against the milestone's data and lists those that fired (`docs/milestone-reviews.md`). An entry is reopened only that way or by a new finding of fact, never by a preference stated again. An entry decided before 2026-11-13 gains its condition when it is next touched, the strict choices first. `test_decided_entries_name_what_would_reopen_them` in `compiler/mente-cli/tests/register.rs` rejects an entry decided on or after 2026-11-13 that has none, a day written in any other form, and an entry dated before 2026-11-13 that the test's list of earlier entries does not hold.
- **Library first** ([`feature-admission`](design-review.md#feature-admission)). A domain is served by functions, types, effects and `comptime`, and syntax is added only for what a library cannot express or check, which the proposing entry shows.
- **Admission by evidence** ([`feature-admission`](design-review.md#feature-admission)). An entry that adds a mechanism names the benchmark tasks it should help, the margin by which it should help them, and its cost in words of the language card or of a topic. At the milestone's review, a mechanism whose tasks are solved no better with it than without leaves the card and is marked here as on trial, its spec claims keeping the labels the evidence rule gives them; it is removed at the next milestone unless its entry is reopened with new evidence. A mechanism that exists for safety or for strictness, the first two aims of `philosophy.md` section 2, is judged on the adversarial tasks and on repairs per solved run, never on solve rates: strict rules are expected to raise a first attempt's failures, which is the hypothesis of `philosophy.md` section 3, and are not removed for doing so.
- **Invariants and the kernel** ([`evolution-invariants`](design-review.md#evolution-invariants), [`elaboration-to-core`](design-review.md#elaboration-to-core)). An entry that proposes a change says which invariants of `philosophy.md` it touches, normally none; changing an invariant takes an entry of its own. An entry that proposes a primitive of the core calculus says why no elaboration into the kernel exists (`core-calculus.md`, item 24).

| `error-conversion` | Inside a function returning `T ! E2`, the spec does not say whether `f()!`, where `f` returns `U ! E1`, needs a conversion from `E1` to `E2`, or how such a conversion is written and found. | **Decided (Norman, 2026-11-11):** postfix `!` propagates only the enclosing function's own error type. | `overflow-check-cost` | `overflow-release` makes integer and fixed-point arithmetic trap on overflow in every build unless Tier 1 proves it absent. Most checks cost little, since the branch is almost never taken and is well predicted, but in a tight loop a check on every step blocks vectorisation and the reordering of arithmetic, and C and Rust release builds do not pay it at all. Every operator is checked on its own, so an intermediate result traps even when the final value fits: `(lo + hi) / 2`, the overflow that once broke Java's `Arrays.binarySearch`, traps although its result always fits. Nothing in the spec says how the cost is kept down, and the trap is implicitly precise: it happens at the operation that overflowed. | Keep trapping on overflow; making release builds wrap would turn overflow into a silent wrong result, worst of all in code no person reads. Lower the cost with two semantic rules, decided with the core calculus in M0, and four measures. **Rule 1, exact intermediates:** within an expression, intermediate results are computed exactly in a wider integer and checked once, where the value is narrowed to its type: a binding, a store, an argument, a return. This removes checks, one per expression rather than one per operator; it costs nothing for 32-bit and narrower types and for fixed point on 64-bit processors, and little for 64-bit types, whose 128-bit intermediates the hardware supports; it never changes a value a program would have produced, and only turns some traps into the mathematically correct result; and it makes run-time arithmetic match the mathematical integers Tier 1 reasons about. Shifts and bit operations are narrowing points, `wrap<T>` and `sat<T>` keep their own rules, and `i128` falls back to a check per operator. It follows CERT's "As-if Infinitely Ranged" integer model. **Rule 2, imprecise trap regions:** within a region that has no externally visible effect, the remaining checks may be merged and deferred to the region's end, so that a loop accumulates an overflow flag, or a vector loop combines its lanes' overflow masks, and checks once, and vectorisation is not blocked. A trap ends the program, so memory written before it that no one has seen is unobservable; a region therefore ends before every externally visible effect: an effect operation, an FFI call, or a write another task can see (`Shared<T>`, atomics, `Channel.send`). Mente's effects and the Law of Exclusivity tell the compiler where those are, which a C compiler cannot know; Ada's standard grants a similar freedom to move language-defined checks (RM 11.6). Together the two rules say: a value traps only when it must fit a type and does not, at its narrowing point or before it becomes visible. A trap then names its region rather than its operation, and replaying the same input on the reference interpreter finds the operation; the three-way differential counts a trap anywhere in the same region as agreement. `@branchless` is unchanged, since a deferred check is still a branch. The four measures: (1) from M1, Tier 1's intervals remove checks in all code, refined or not, as an optimisation rather than an obligation, so `gradual-verification` holds; (2) loop versioning and checks hoisted out of loops, in MIR from M5 and deeper in stage 3, where one test before a loop selects an unchecked version when the whole loop provably cannot overflow; (3) from M2, `cost-report` gives each check left in a hot path the contract or refinement that would remove it, a prescription an agent can apply where a person would not bother; (4) the cost is measured as a figure of its own from M1 (`performance-stage`). What can be neither proven nor merged keeps its check, and code that wants no check writes `wrap<T>` or `sat<T>`. Deciding this entry changes `refinement-and-fixed-point.md` item 5 to say when and where a trap occurs. **Amended when decided (2026-11-08): exact intermediates beyond 128 bits.** Rule 1 as written assumes every intermediate fits a 128-bit integer, but a product of three `u64` values can reach about 2^192, and only `i128` was given a fallback. So: an operation whose range, computed from the static types alone, may not fit in 128 bits is itself a narrowing point, where the exact result is checked against its operands' type, as it would be without Rule 1. Rule 1's guarantee still holds (no value a program would have produced changes; some traps become the mathematically correct result), and where a check falls depends only on the types, never on run-time values, so it stays deterministic and predictable. | done: [`refinement-and-fixed-point.md`](../spec/refinement-and-fixed-point.md) item 5 (Norman, 2026-11-08, with the amendment to Rule 1 at the end of the resolution; commit "Apply the decisions made before M0 to the roadmap, the spec and the brief") |

Whether `val x: u8 = y`, with `y` a `u32`, needs an explicit conversion was not said. | No conversions between integer types: an integer value is a mathematical integer and an integer type names a range, so any integer expression flows into any integer type and is checked where it is narrowed. A plain type is a range checked at run time where Tier 1 cannot prove it; a refined type is a range proven at compile time. Wrapping and saturation stay explicit (`wrap<T>`, `sat<T>`). | done: [`core-calculus.md`](../spec/core-calculus.md) item 2 (Norman, 2026-11-08; commit "Write the core calculus of M1") |
| `integer-inference` | With integers as mathematical values, `u8 + u32` is well defined, but an unannotated binding such as `val s = a + b` still needs a type, and none was specified. | When an expression's integer operands share a type, an unannotated binding takes it (`u8 + u8` is a `u8`, trapping above 255 at the binding); a literal takes its neighbours' type, and literals alone are `i64`; when the operands' types differ, the binding states its type, and the diagnostic suggests the smallest standard type that holds both ranges. | done: [`core-calculus.md`](../spec/core-calculus.md) item 5 (Norman, 2026-11-08; commit "Write the core calculus of M1") |
| `literal-type-from-operands` | Item 5 of `core-calculus.md` said "An integer literal takes the type of the other operands, or of the place it is narrowed into", and the three execution paths read it three ways where an operation works in a type, under `~`, `&`, `|`, `^` and a shift (review of pull request 138). With `a: u8`: `a & ~3` into a `u8` gave 252 on the reference interpreter and trapped on -4 in MIR and native code; into a `u16` the interpreter trapped on 65532, taking the place's type before `a`'s; `a + ~3` into a `u16` gave 65533 on the interpreter, stopped MIR as broken and gave 253 in native code; `val m: u8 = ~3`, `mask(~3)`, `P(m: ~3)` and `m &= ~1` gave 252 or 254 on the interpreter and trapped elsewhere; `if c { ~3 } else { 4 }` into a `u8` trapped on all three; `[~3, 1]` into a `[2]u8` trapped on the interpreter and was not lowered; `1 << 63` into a `u64` gave 2^63 on the interpreter and trapped elsewhere. | **Decided (Norman, 2026-11-14):** a literal's type comes only from the other operands of its expression, and with none it is `i64`; the place a value is narrowed into never gives a literal its type. So `a & ~3` is `a & 252` and `a + ~3` is `a + 252` wherever the value goes, `m &= ~1` takes `m`'s type since it is `m = m & ~1`, `~3` alone is -4, and `val m: u8 = ~3` narrows -4 into `u8`, an error (`E0508`) whose machine-applicable fix writes `~3_u8`; a literal landing in a place of an integer type with no operation working in a type is an exact integer narrowed there, so `val m: u8 = 3` is unchanged. A subexpression means one thing wherever it lands, and types flow one way, up from the operands, so the three paths read the checker's types and none derives a type from a destination, which is where they had disagreed; the price is a suffix in a few spellings, and brevity comes last among the aims. Go's untyped constants behave this way. Rejected: reading A, the other operands first and then the place, which keeps `val m: u8 = ~3` but gives a literal's type two sources; and reading B, the place first, under which `a & ~3` traps in a `u16` place and not in a `u8` one. **Decided (Norman, 2026-11-15):** a comparison gives a literal the other operand's type too, so the rule is one: a literal takes the type of the other operand of the operation it stands in, a comparison included, and an expression of literals alone is an `i64`, two of them compared included. The other operand is the operand of that operation, an expression too: in `a + 1 == 300` the literal takes the type of `a + 1`. With `a: u8`, `a == ~3` compares `a` with 252, and `a == 300`, `a == -1` and `a < 256` are errors (`E0233`), in a condition, a `guard` and a contract clause alike, whose fix spells an ordering in range where the other operand's values stay in its type (`a <= 255`, machine-applicable), gives an equality there none, since it is constant and is to be rewritten, and otherwise states the literal's type with a suffix (`a + b <= 300_u16`); two operands of different types that are not literals still compare exactly. Rejected: the comparison as it was, which gave a literal no type, so that `a == ~3` compared `a` with -4 and was silently always false; and no type with an error for a literal outside the other operand's type, which is two rules where one serves. Made exact the same day (Norman, 2026-11-15): a literal takes the type of every operand that has one of the arithmetic or bit expression it stands in, the smallest standard type that holds them all, the one `E0212` suggests, up to a comparison's side, a call's argument, a narrowing point, a branch's value or an index, and a bit operation with an operand of a type or a shift whose shifted operand has one, which works in that type; so with `a: u8` and `b: u32`, `a + b + ~3` and `a + ~3 + b` both add 4294967292, and the order of the operands never changes a literal's value. Rejected: the operation the literal stands in alone, under which `a + ~3 + b` added 252, the order changing the value. | `tuned-library-kernels` | In real programs the last percent often sit in a few routines that every program calls: copying and comparing memory, hashing, searching bytes, sorting, validating UTF-8, and parsing and printing numbers.
